Speaker Script — LED runs in SNOLAB Run 7

Slide 1 — Title(about 15 s)

Hi everyone, I'm Zhiheng. Today: the LED runs in Run 7, after the Cf calibration. Four parts, as Prof. Liu asked: who sees which LED, is the amplitude stable, is the trigger stable, and the pulse templates.

Slide 2 — The data(about 55 s)

The data: 45 LED series, from September 15 to 21. Each run waits about 880 seconds, then the LEDs turn on. Phase one: 15 LEDs flash together. Three seconds off. Phase two: 12 other LEDs. Each LED flashes once per second, exactly 151 times per phase. One thing to keep in mind: each trigger reads out only the detector that triggered.

Slide 3 — Which LED made the trigger(about 70 s)

The data do not record which LED flashed. We only have the trigger time and the detector. But each LED flashes at a fixed time within the second. Take the trigger time modulo one second, and every LED is one peak. Detectors that see the same flash trigger within 0.2 milliseconds of each other. The script tells us which LEDs are on. If the LED's own detector is in the peak, the peak is that LED; if not, we use elimination. Sometimes two LEDs flash at the same moment and cannot be separated. That happens in 22 groups; we leave them out.

Slide 4 — Part 1 — who triggers on which LED: phase 1(about 70 s)

This plot answers: when an LED flashes, which detector triggers? Columns are the 15 LEDs of phase one, rows are detectors. The number is the fraction of the 151 flashes on which that detector triggers, averaged over 45 series. The red box is the LED's own detector; most are 1.00. There are numbers outside the red boxes too: a neighbour also sees the light. Every cell with a number has an ID, like P1-07. We use it for the templates later.

Slide 5 — Part 1 — who triggers on which LED: phase 2(about 45 s)

Phase two, the same picture with 12 LEDs. The same pattern: the own detector triggers on almost every flash, and a neighbour often does too. Together the two phases have 43 cells.

Slide 6 — Part 1 — what we learn(about 70 s)

What we learn from part one. First, light reaches only the detector with the adjacent number, nothing further. 18 of the 43 cells are a neighbour, and none skips a number. Second, no pair crosses a group: all pairs are inside detectors 1 to 6, 7 to 12, 13 to 18, or 19 to 24. Third, the direction is fixed: LED 0 and 1 light the detector with the number below, LED 3, 4 and 5 the one above. At the end of a group there is no neighbour, so those LEDs are seen only by their own detector. There are two exceptions, listed below. Finally, only the triggering detector is read out, so light below threshold is invisible. These numbers are lower limits.

Slide 7 — Part 2 — amplitude vs time(about 65 s)

Part two, amplitude over time. Each row is one cell, each column one series, in time order. The colour is that series' amplitude divided by the row's median. White means normal. Most of it is white: 72.5 percent of the points are within 5 percent, 90.8 percent within 10 percent. So it is mostly stable. Most cells also go down slowly; the median is -0.9 percent per day. A few spots stand out. We look at them in a moment.

Slide 8 — Part 2 — whose change is it: the LED or the detector?(about 60 s)

The amplitude changes. Is it the LED, or the detector? We can check. Left: one LED, seen by two detectors. The two lines move together. Middle: one detector, with its two different LEDs. The lines do not move together. Right: all the pairs. Blue is one LED on two detectors: the correlation is mostly above 0.9. Orange is one detector with its two LEDs: mostly near zero. So the changes come mainly from the LED light, not from the detectors.

Slide 9 — Part 2 — cases to look at again(about 75 s)

These need a second look. The clearest is the early morning of September 18, 3 series in a row: Z20 is at only 0.25–0.26 of normal. Z19, looking at Z20's LED, is at 0.56; but Z19 with its own LED is normal. So Z20's LEDs were dim. The next run had no LED flashes at all, and by 1 pm it was back to normal. The LED settings in the ODB did not change. Also, on the first day a few cells are about 20 percent high, and then come down.

Slide 10 — Part 3 — trigger fraction vs time(about 80 s)

Part three, trigger fraction over time. Again one row per cell, one column per series. Yellow means it triggers on every flash. The own-LED rows stay yellow: stable. Other rows show steps: from zero to one, or back. I checked the ODB: the steps are exactly where the trigger thresholds were changed, the black lines. There are 6 different threshold settings during the week. For example Z9 and Z10 trigger only in periods 2 and 4, which is when their thresholds were low. So the steps are settings, not unstable detectors.

Slide 11 — Part 4 — one template per cell, made like the Z7 template(about 75 s)

Part four, templates. One template per cell, all 45 series together. The method is the same as for the Z7 template. Each pulse is low-passed, baseline-subtracted, divided by its peak, and fitted with two exponentials with a free start time. Pulses are aligned on the fitted start, then averaged. One extra step: when an LED is driven, the readout picks up a very narrow spike. A short median filter removes it first. At the end we fit an analytic function to the template: one rise, two falls. This is a clean example, Z2 with its own LED: the residual is nearly flat.

Slide 12 — Part 4 — own LED: pickup of the LED drive pulse(about 55 s)

But some own-LED cells have a problem at the start of the pulse. Take Z7: several channels show a fast step at the start, before the phonon pulse. This is the LED drive pulse, picked up by the detector's own readout. A neighbour looking at the same LED does not show it. 11 cells have it, all own-LED. For these, the start of the template cannot be used as it is. We can use the neighbour's template, or cut the start before fitting.

Slide 13 — Part 4 — all 43 cells(about 50 s)

All 43 cells, one small panel each: the detector template in red, the analytic pulse in black dashes. The twelve-channel plot of every cell is in the backup, and so are the fitted time constants.

Slide 14 — Part 4 — example pulses: Z7 PSum with Z7's LED(about 75 s)

Prof. Liu wanted to see how the pulse shape changes with time. This is Z7 with its own LED, all twelve channels summed: the PSum. One typical pulse per series, 45 in total. Light to dark is early to late. Left: raw ADC, the whole 52 milliseconds. Middle: zoom. The negative spike at the start is the LED pickup from before. In the middle you can see the amplitude going down from light to dark: that is the slow decline of this LED from part two. Right: spike removed, each pulse divided by its own peak, so only the shape is left. The pulses of the week lie nearly on top of each other: the shape did not change.

Slide 15 — Summary, and what comes next(about 65 s)

To sum up. One: every LED trigger is traced to one LED. Light reaches only the adjacent detector, in a fixed direction. Two: amplitudes are mostly within 10 percent, and the changes come mainly from the LEDs. Z20's LEDs were dim on the morning of September 18; we need to find out why. Three: the steps in the trigger fraction are threshold settings. Four: all 43 cells have a template and an analytic pulse, made like the Z7 template. A few detectors pick up their own LED drive; watch out for those. Next: find out what happened to Z20 in those hours, handle the pickup cells, and agree on how these templates will be used.

Likely questions (one or two sentences each)

Q1. Why no detector further away?

Only the triggering detector is read out. If light arrives but stays below threshold, we cannot see it. So the plot is a lower limit.

Q2. What if two LEDs flash at the same time?

The LEDs are switched on one after the other, so sometimes two of them flash within 0.2 milliseconds. Time alone cannot separate them, so we do not use that group. There are 22 such groups.

Q3. Does the median filter change the pulse shape?

Hardly. It removes only spikes narrower than 18 microseconds. The phonon rise is slower than that, so the peak and the rise time stay the same.

Q4. Was the Z20 drop the LED or the detector?

It looks like the LED. Z19 also saw less of Z20's light, while Z19 with its own LED was normal. We do not know the cause yet; we need the logs of those hours.

Q5. Who changed the trigger thresholds?

I do not know. I only see the thresholds in the ODB at the start of each run; there are 6 settings during the week. We should ask the people on shift.

Q6. What changed with the 0918 script?

The LED settings are identical. It should switch off Z17's L1 trigger. But the ODB shows Z17's trigger on in every run, and Z17 keeps triggering.

Q7. How is the amplitude defined?

Remove the spikes, subtract the baseline, take the maximum, in ADC. Then the median per series, divided by the median over all series.

Q8. Why groups of six?

That is what the data show: no pair crosses 6 and 7, 12 and 13, or 18 and 19. The number of readout boards in the ODB splits the same way.
