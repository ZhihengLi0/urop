Speaker Script — LED runs in SNOLAB Run 7

Slide 1: Title(about 15 s)

Hi everyone, I'm Zhiheng. Today I'll show the post-activation LED data from Run 7. Four points: who sees which LED, amplitude stability, trigger stability, and pulse templates.

Slide 2: Background(about 45 s)

First, the background. This is Run 7 data after the neutron activation. The DAQ script runs in a loop: low-background data, then one LED run, about every 2 hours. Each line on the top is one LED run. From September 15 to 21 there are 47 of them, and we use 45.

Slide 3: Example LED run(about 40 s)

Here is one LED run as an example. In phase one, 15 LEDs flash. In phase two, 12 LEDs flash. Each LED flashes once per second, 151 times in total. Each trigger reads out only the detector that triggered.

Slide 4: How a trigger is matched to an LED(about 40 s)

How do we know which LED a trigger comes from? Each LED flashes once per second, and each LED has its own time within the second. So we plot the trigger time within its second, and each LED is one peak. A trigger in a peak belongs to that LED. The script tells us which LEDs are on, and the LED's own detector triggers in its peak, so we know which LED each peak is. The few triggers outside the peaks are background. If two LEDs flash within 0.2 milliseconds, we cannot separate them. We leave those out, and they are grey in the later plots.

Slide 5: Visibility: phase 1(about 55 s)

This is the visibility plot for phase one. Columns are LEDs, rows are detectors. The number is how often the detector triggers when that LED flashes. The red box is the LED's own detector, and it is almost always one. A number next to a red box means the neighbouring detector also sees the light.

Slide 6: Visibility: phase 2(about 35 s)

Phase two shows the same pattern. The own detector triggers almost every time, and often the next detector too. The light only reaches the next detector, never further.

Slide 7: Stability: amplitude vs time(about 50 s)

Second, amplitude versus time. Each row is one detector with one LED, each column is one series, in time order. Phase one is on the left, phase two on the right. White means average. Most of it is white: 91 percent of the points are within 10 percent. So it is mostly stable, but a few spots need a second look.

Slide 8: Amplitude: cases to look at again(about 55 s)

These need a second look. The clearest one: early on September 18, Z20 is much lower, and Z19 with Z20's LED is lower too. A few hours later they are back. The operation elog says that on September 17 at 10 pm there were chiller alarms, and the detectors were put into safe state. So some HEMT settings of Z19 and Z20 might have changed. The small red bars are the error bars. Also, on the first day a few cells are a bit high.

Slide 9: Stability: trigger fraction vs time(about 55 s)

Third, trigger fraction versus time. Yellow means it triggers on every flash. The own-LED rows stay yellow: stable. Other rows have steps. The steps are exactly where the trigger thresholds were changed, the black lines. For example, the elog says that on September 18 at ten past midnight the thresholds of detectors 9, 10, 11 and 15 were raised. So these are setting changes, not unstable detectors.

Slide 10: Template generation: method(about 65 s)

Fourth, templates. First, how they are made. Step one: select the events where this detector triggered on this LED's flash, and use all twelve channels. Step two: a very short median filter removes the narrow spike from the LED drive. Step three: a 100 kilohertz low-pass filter. Step four: subtract the baseline and divide by the peak. Step five: fit each pulse with two exponentials, with a free start time. The top right plot shows one pulse and its fit. Step six: shift each measured pulse so that the fitted start is at the same time. Step seven: average all aligned pulses of the 45 series. That is the template, the red line at the bottom right. The fit is only used to find the start time; the template itself is the mean of the measured pulses. Finally we fit the template with one rise and two falls to get the analytic pulse.

Slide 11: Templates: example(about 30 s)

Here is Z2 as an example, twelve channels. Blue are the aligned single pulses, red is the template, and the dashed black line is the analytic pulse. Channels with a grey title have no clear pulse, so we do not fit an analytic pulse there.

Slide 12: Example pulses: Z7 PSum with Z7's LED(about 55 s)

We want to see how the pulse shape changes with time. This is Z7 with its own LED, all twelve channels summed: the PSum. One pulse per series, 45 in total. Light to dark is early to late: yellow is the first day, purple the last. Each pulse goes through a 40 kilohertz low-pass filter to remove high-frequency noise, and is divided by its own peak, so we only see the shape. The pulses lie on top of each other: the shape did not change during the week. The narrow spike just before the pulse is pickup from the LED drive.

Slide 13: Summary(about 40 s)

To sum up. Visibility: each LED is seen by its own detector, and often by the next one. Amplitude: mostly stable; Z19 and Z20 were low on September 18, after the detectors were put into safe state. Trigger fraction: stable; the steps are threshold changes. Templates: one for every cell, and the Z7 pulse shape did not change during the week. Thank you.

Likely questions (one or two sentences each)

Q1. Why no detector further away?

Only the triggering detector is read out. If the light is below threshold, we cannot see it.

Q2. Why are Z19 and Z20 low on 18 September?

The operation elog says that on 17 September at 22:04 there were chiller alarms and the detectors were put into safe state. After the recovery, some HEMT settings of Z19 and Z20 might have changed. By 1 pm it was back to normal.

Q3. Who changed the trigger thresholds?

It is in the elog. For example, on 18 September at 00:10 the thresholds of Det09, Det10, Det11 and Det15 were raised. I still need to look up the other changes.

Q4. How exactly are the templates made?

For each pulse: median filter, 100 kilohertz low-pass, subtract the baseline, divide by the peak, fit two exponentials to find the start, and shift the measured pulse to that start. Then we average the pulses of all series. The fit is only used to find the start.

Q5. Some templates have a spike at the start?

Those are own-LED cells: the LED drive is picked up by the readout. The neighbouring detector sees the same LED cleanly, so we can use its template.

Q6. Why the 40 kHz low-pass on the example pulses?

To remove high-frequency noise, so the 45 pulses are easier to compare. The pulse rise and fall are much slower than that, so the shape hardly changes.
